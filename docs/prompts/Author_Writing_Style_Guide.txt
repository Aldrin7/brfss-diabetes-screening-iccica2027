===========================================================================
   AUTHOR WRITING STYLE GUIDE (TOPIC-NEUTRAL, REUSABLE)
   Derived from a published empirical ML paper (MDPI "World", 2026)
===========================================================================
Purpose: Tell an AI how to draft new manuscripts so they read like this
author's published work. It governs STRUCTURE, VOICE, and RECURRING
MOVES only. It never overrides the integrity rules of the Unified Master
Prompt: a style rule must not be used to imply a result, a citation, or a
validation that was not actually obtained. Do not copy sentences from the
source paper; reproduce the style, not the text.

Companion: Unified_Research_Paper_Master_Prompt.txt (append this guide to
it as "Part 8B", or paste it right after Part 8).


---------------------------------------------------------------------------
1. OVERALL IDENTITY OF THE STYLE
---------------------------------------------------------------------------
- Formal, cautious, policy-aware academic prose. First-person plural
  ("we") for decisions and acknowledgments; passive voice for procedures.
- The paper positions itself as a SCREENING / DIAGNOSTIC / EXPLORATORY
  tool, never as a causal, forecasting, or "policy-ready" instrument.
- Honesty about limits is a feature of the voice: limitations are
  announced in the section where the choice is made, then consolidated
  in a dedicated limitations subsection.
- Recurring thesis pattern: "complements, not replaces" existing expert
  or mechanistic methods; "associations, not causal effects";
  "hypothesis-generating, not diagnostic".
- Practical or societal framing (e.g., policy use, SDG alignment) appears
  in the abstract, introduction, discussion, and conclusion.


---------------------------------------------------------------------------
2. DOCUMENT SKELETON (MIRROR THIS ORDER AND NUMBERING)
---------------------------------------------------------------------------
TITLE      "Topic Phrase: An Interpretable [Method] Framework for [Task A]
           and [Task B]" -- colon structure, descriptive, no hype words.

ABSTRACT   One dense paragraph (about 250-300 words, or the venue limit),
           in this fixed sequence:
             1. Problem context and why it matters (1-2 sentences)
             2. The need ("Practitioners require tools that are
                reproducible, transparent, grounded in open data")
             3. What this study presents, data scope (counts, years),
                model with software version
             4. Training vs. held-out evaluation design
             5. Features used, with one-clause caveats on proxies
             6. Headline metrics (actual numbers with units)
             7. Ablation findings
             8. One "Notably," sentence on a surprising or humbling
                finding (e.g., a simple baseline matching the complex
                model)
             9. Explainability finding
            10. Sensitivity/scenario finding, labeled "stylized"
            11. Diagnostic / residual finding
            12. Explicit scope sentence: "designed as a ... rather than a
                validated causal or forecasting instrument"
            13. Complement-not-replace statement and societal-goal link
           Keywords: 8-10, from broad to specific.

1. INTRODUCTION (numbered subsections)
   1.1 Problem context and practical/policy requirements
       - Close with a numbered or (i)-(iv) list of what a usable tool
         must satisfy.
   1.2 Determinants of the outcome: what drives it beyond the headline
       factor (mechanisms, then non-primary drivers)
   1.3 Methodological approaches: comparative strengths AND limitations
       of each family (process-based / statistical / ML)
   1.4 Interpretability, transparency, and credibility for consequential
       use; state the design choices that respond (validation design,
       explainability method)
   1.5 Research objectives and contributions
       - "This paper addresses three interrelated research questions:"
         numbered list, each with a bold-style topic label and a
         colon ("Yield driver identification: What ...?")
       - "We have made the following contributions: (i) ...; (ii) ...;
         (iii) ...; (iv) ..." using roman numerals in one sentence
       - End with the positioning sentence (screening-level, complements
         expert and causal work)

2. MATERIALS AND METHODS (numbered, short, single-purpose subsections)
   2.1 Data sources and scope (+ a "Scope and limitations of [unit of
       analysis]" paragraph)
   2.2 Variables (+ Table 1: Variable / Description / Unit / Source; and a
       caveat paragraph with (i)(ii)(iii) on proxy variables)
   2.3 Data preparation and exploratory characterization
       - sample-flow schematic in one sentence with arrows:
         "N raw → n excluded for X → N final"
       - missingness structure examined; representativeness caveat
       - encoding choices stated with their trade-off
   2.4 Feature engineering (bullets for each constructed feature, then a
       bold-style "Temporal validity" statement explaining no leakage)
   2.5 Validation strategy (blockwise/temporal split; why random split
       would be optimistic; acknowledge single-split limitation and point
       forward to the future-work section)
   2.6 Model selection: primary + benchmarks, with one sentence on why the
       simplest baseline is methodologically important
   2.7 Hyperparameter optimization (search method, folds, bullet list of
       search spaces, honest note on search depth and CV design)
   2.8 Evaluation framework: metrics defined with one-clause rationale
       each; bullet list of ablation variants; "does not constitute
       external validation" sentence
   2.9 Explainability method (+ stability check description + an
       "Interpretive boundaries" paragraph on multicollinearity and
       approximation uncertainty)
   2.10 Scenario / perturbation analysis (bullets for each scenario +
       a "Critical caveat" paragraph)
   2.11 Software implementation and transparency (languages, packages,
       versions)

3. RESULTS (numbered; interpretation labeled and separated)
   3.1 Predictive performance and final configuration (bullets of the
       headline metrics with units)
   3.2 Ablation (Table 2 = specification vs. metric, sorted best to
       worst), then an "Interpretation and implications" paragraph
   3.3 Explainability findings as a numbered list with bold-style lead-ins
   3.4 Residual / diagnostic analysis (Table 3), then an "Interpretive
       caution" paragraph
   3.5 Scenario analysis (the formula in display form, Table 4), then a
       "Caveats and boundary conditions" paragraph

4. DISCUSSION
   4.1 What the model is capturing (and what it is not)
   4.2 Contextual positioning within the existing literature
   4.3 Practical deployment scenarios (numbered, each ending with the
       corroboration requirement)
   4.4 Methodological limitations and validity boundary conditions
       (numbered, each with a bold-style topic label: spatial
       aggregation / data currency / variable coarseness / scenario
       validity / correlation vs. causation / validation completeness)
   4.5 Prospects for future development (bold-style labels: temporal
       extension, feature enrichment, better inputs, validation rigor,
       uncertainty quantification, hybrid approaches)

5. CONCLUSIONS
   - One-sentence restatement of what was done.
   - "Key substantive findings are:" numbered, each with a bold-style
     lead-in label and 2-3 sentences.
   - Closing paragraph labeled "Appropriate use context", ending with a
     short contrast sentence ("It is a screening tool, not a ...").

BACK MATTER (MDPI-style order): Author Contributions (CRediT, initials),
Funding, Institutional Review Board Statement, Informed Consent
Statement, Data Availability Statement (URL + access date),
Acknowledgments, Conflicts of Interest, Abbreviations table, References,
Publisher's note.

3. VOICE AND SENTENCE-LEVEL HABITS
---------------------------------------------------------------------------
Characteristic constructions (use freely):
  - Hedged verbs: "suggests", "indicates", "is consistent with",
    "corroborates", "may reflect", "plausibly explained by".
  - Acknowledgment openers: "We acknowledge that...", "We note that...",
    "We caution against...", "We emphasize several caveats...".
  - Contrast framing: "X does not imply Y; rather, it reflects Z."
  - Dual-label terms: "hypothesis-generating diagnostic flags",
    "stylized sensitivity probes", "screening-level diagnostics",
    "conditional predictive associations".
  - Enumerations inline with (i), (ii), (iii) inside a sentence, and
    numbered lists for research questions, findings, and limitations.
  - Cross-references by section number ("discussed in Section 4.4",
    "future extension (Section 4.5)").
  - Bold-style lead-in phrases at the start of a paragraph to flag its
    function: "Temporal validity.", "Critical caveat:", "Interpretive
    boundaries.", "Interpretation and implications.", "Caveats and
    boundary conditions.", "Appropriate use context."
  - Citations as numbered brackets in order of first appearance, with
    ranges: [1-7], [4,6,7].
  - Long, multi-clause sentences with parenthetical examples
    ("(e.g., irrigated vs. rainfed systems, regional drought pockets)").
  - Units spaced from values (11,183 hg/ha); degree symbol (+1 °C);
    true minus signs (−20%); en-dash ranges (1990–2013).
  - Tables: caption ABOVE. Figures: caption BELOW, multi-panel as
    "(a) ... (b) ...".

Tone rules:
  - No "novel", "state-of-the-art", "groundbreaking", "policy-ready".
  - Describe what was done rather than praising it.
  - Always pair a positive result with the sentence that bounds it.


---------------------------------------------------------------------------
4. RECURRING RHETORICAL MOVES (REUSE THE MOVE, NOT THE WORDS)
---------------------------------------------------------------------------
 1. The humble-baseline move: include a simple baseline on purpose and
    report honestly if it matches the complex model; then reinterpret the
    contribution accordingly.
 2. The ablation-as-explanation move: remove feature groups and report the
    absolute drop in the metric, with one sentence on what the drop means.
 3. The "does not mean X" move: pre-empt misreadings of a negative or weak
    result (e.g., weak signal at coarse resolution does not mean the
    factor is unimportant).
 4. The proxy-caveat move: whenever a variable stands in for a concept,
    state what it captures and what it misses, in (i)(ii)(iii) form.
 5. The association-not-causation move: repeat once per analysis type
    (model, attribution, scenario, residuals), tailored to each.
 6. The stylized-scenario move: label all perturbation analyses as
    sensitivity probes, bounded by the historical feature range, not
    projections.
 7. The residuals-as-hypotheses move: treat systematic errors as flags for
    investigation, never as diagnoses.
 8. The complement-not-replace close: end sections and the paper by
    placing the tool alongside expert, subnational, mechanistic, and
    causal methods.
 9. The currency move: state the data period and say what has changed
    since, and what updated data would be used next.

5. SUGGESTED REFINEMENTS (KEEP THE STYLE, FIX COMMON WEAK SPOTS)
---------------------------------------------------------------------------
These keep the voice while aligning it with the integrity and rulebook
requirements in the Unified Master Prompt.

 a) Say each caveat once in full, then refer back by section number.
    The same distribution-shift / "screening tool" sentence should not
    appear in four places.
 b) Support every "we found / no pattern identified / results were
    similar" sentence with a number or table (e.g., report the spread
    across replicates, not just "similar").
 c) Make sure every number in figures, tables, text, and abstract is
    identical. After finalizing, search for each headline metric and
    confirm all occurrences match, including numbers printed inside
    figures.
 d) Make sure the figure count in text matches what is plotted ("six most
    impactful features" must show six).
 e) Report variance (mean ± std over seeds/folds) and significance tests
    for any comparison; label single-run numbers as pilot.
 f) Do not claim robustness or "strength of attribution patterns" from a
    small number of replicates; describe what was done and let the
    reader judge.
 g) Prefer plain verbs over stacked intensifiers ("substantially",
    "pronounced", "precipitates substantial degradation" -> "R² fell
    from 0.982 to 0.940").
 h) Avoid vague quantifiers ("overwhelming share") unless the share is
    computed and reported.
 i) Check mojibake and symbol corruption in equations (e.g., a Δ or ×
    rendering as stray characters) before submission; render equations
    properly.
 j) Use defined terms consistently (e.g., "decile" vs. "top ten
    countries"; "temporal validation" vs. "test set").
 k) Keep every claim about what was run in the Methods identical to what
    the code actually did (CV scheme, encodings, sample counts, which
    exclusions overlap and by how much).
