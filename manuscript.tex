\begin{abstract}
Machine learning models fitted to public health survey data carry failure modes that are invisible in headline accuracy. This paper audits an interpretable screening framework for diabetes on the 2015 Behavioral Risk Factor Surveillance System (BRFSS; $n = 253{,}680$, 21 features) and reports what the audits change. Logistic regression, random forest, and histogram-based gradient boosting are compared over five seeds, and three held-out protocols bound duplicate-profile leakage. Leakage is small but not nil: the deduplicated test protocol lowers gradient-boosting AUC from $0.8269$ to $0.8177$, and 1{,}963 rows share a covariate vector with a contradicting label. Class-balancing weights cost $60\times$ in calibration (ECE $0.2367$ versus $0.0038$ unweighted) while buying no discrimination (AUC $0.8265$ versus $0.8267$); trained unweighted, the model needs no recalibration and reaches slightly higher net benefit. Fairness gaps are quantified with bootstrap intervals and group supports: the sex TPR gap is $0.0171$, whereas the age gap of $0.5111$ persists at every operating point tested and rises to $0.79$ out of time. Attribute-specific thresholds equalize their target attribute in sample but do not transfer: the income gap moves from $0.0899$ to $0.8647$ by 2021. We therefore report a global sensitivity-targeted threshold, document the mitigation that fails, and state where the data cannot support population-level claims.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

\subsection{Problem context and practical requirements}
Diabetes is among the most prevalent chronic diseases globally, and a substantial fraction of cases are undiagnosed. Population screening can surface at-risk individuals, but any such tool must be reproducible on open data, transparent in how predictions are formed, honest about what it does not capture, and explicitly bounded against use for individual clinical diagnosis. This paper audits a survey-based screening tool against these requirements. Beyond age and body mass index, diabetes risk reflects blood pressure, cholesterol, physical activity, diet, alcohol use, mental health, and socioeconomic conditions; many of these are self-reported, which makes them accessible but noisy and subject to recall bias, and several are consequences rather than antecedents of diabetes, which limits their use for early-detection reasoning.

\subsection{Methodological approaches}
Prior work has applied logistic regression and, more recently, machine learning classifiers to the BRFSS, with AUC values roughly between 0.72 and 0.80 \cite{xie2019}. Related work has expanded the evidence base in three directions: non-invasive dysglycemia screening on NHANES with calibration and decision-curve analysis \cite{sun2026beyond}; large-scale external validation of NHANES-trained diabetes models on BRFSS, documenting a $9.7\%$ relative AUC drop under distribution shift and marked age-related disparities \cite{pall2026comprehensive}; and parsimonious feature selection on BRFSS, reducing 21 features to 10 with a copula tail-dependence filter at AUC $0.823$ \cite{aich2026copula}.

\subsection{Why audits change the conclusions}
Reported accuracy is a weak summary of a screening model, and widely used claims about survey-based diabetes models are not reproducible under a leakage-audited protocol: on this same 253{,}680-row dataset, published pipelines report AUC near $0.99$ \cite{pall2026comprehensive}, whereas we measure $0.827$. We therefore audit rather than chase AUC, and three of our findings are negative results that change practice rather than confirm it: class-balancing weights are unnecessary and costly, attribute-specific thresholds do not transfer out of time, and the age disparity is not a threshold artifact.

\subsection{Research questions and contributions}
We address three questions: (1) \textbf{Driver identification:} which features most strongly associate with diabetes status, and how stable are these attributions across seeds and survey waves? (2) \textbf{Fairness:} how do performance and error rates differ across sex, age, income, and education groups, how much of each gap is support starvation, and does any mitigation survive validation? (3) \textbf{Deployment utility:} what training recipe, operating point, and calibration does a screening program actually need? Our contributions are: (i) a three-protocol leakage audit with a reconciled duplicate definition and a label-noise sensitivity analysis; (ii) a calibration audit across four calibrators with per-wave transport analysis, showing class weighting to be an unnecessary burden; (iii) fairness quantification with supports, bootstrap inference, design-effect sensitivity, intersectional cells, and a support-restricted age estimate; (iv) an honest evaluation of attribute-specific thresholds, in sample and out of time, showing they do not transfer; (v) SHAP explanations with stability metrics and comparison against alternative selectors and permutation importance; (vi) out-of-time validation on 2017--2021 waves; and (vii) decision-curve net benefit. The tool is positioned as screening-level and complementing, not replacing, clinical assessment.

\section{Materials and Methods}

\subsection{Data source and scope}
We use the CDC Diabetes Health Indicators dataset \cite{cdc2017}, derived from the 2015 BRFSS \cite{brfss}, a telephone survey conducted annually by the CDC since 1984. The cleaned release contains 253{,}680 listwise-complete respondents and 21 features, under CC0 (public domain). The target is \texttt{Diabetes\_binary} (0 = no diabetes, 1 = prediabetes or diabetes; prevalence 13.93\%). Three features of the release constrain our claims. Race/ethnicity is absent, so fairness analysis is limited to sex, age, income, and education. The release carries no survey design variables (weights, strata, PSUs), so population-level claims are not supported and we quantify only the sensitivity of our uncertainty to assumed design effects. Finally, it collapses prediabetes and diabetes, which we address with severity-stratified analysis rather than a condition-level breakdown.

\subsection{Variables}
The 21 features fall into five groups: clinical (HighBP, HighChol, CholCheck, Stroke, HeartDiseaseorAttack, GenHlth, BMI), lifestyle (Smoker, PhysActivity, Fruits, Veggies, HvyAlcoholConsump), access (AnyHealthcare, NoDocbcCost), mental and physical status (MentHlth, PhysHlth, DiffWalk), and demographic (Sex, Age, Education, Income). Several are proxies---general health status stands in for clinical status, income and education for socioeconomic position---and we treat them as proxies throughout, saying so where results depend on them.

\subsection{Data preparation and sample flow}
Raw: 253{,}680 respondents $\rightarrow$ no exclusions (no missing values) $\rightarrow$ 253{,}680 final analytical sample; the \texttt{ID} column was dropped and no row was removed. A stratified 80/20 train/test split (seed 42) yields 202{,}944 train and 50{,}736 test cases, with a 64/16/20 train/calibration/test split for calibration work. All preprocessing is fit on the training portion only.

\subsection{Feature engineering and validation strategy}
Features are used as provided (binary or ordinal integers). Continuous-valued fields (BMI, MentHlth, PhysHlth, GenHlth) are standardized for logistic regression only; tree models use raw values. No derived features are added, keeping the screening questionnaire short. Model development uses stratified 5-fold cross-validation on the training portion and a held-out stratified test set for final reporting, with variance reported across five independent seeds (full re-split and re-train) as mean $\pm$ std. We acknowledge the single-split limitation.

\subsection{Model selection}
Three models: (M0) logistic regression with L2 penalty (interpretable baseline), (M1) random forest (50--100 trees, max depth 20), (M2) scikit-learn's histogram-based gradient boosting (150 iterations, 31 leaves). The humble-baseline move: logistic regression is included deliberately and reported honestly. Because class-balancing weights distort the probability scale, we also train an unweighted gradient-boosting variant, and we report both. Baseline settings are fixed and light; to verify that these do not disadvantage any baseline we run a randomized search (20 iterations for gradient boosting, 6 for random forest, 10 for logistic regression; 3-fold CV on the training portion only).

\subsection{Evaluation framework}
Discrimination: ROC-AUC, average precision (AUPRC), F1, accuracy. Calibration: Brier score, expected calibration error (ECE), and maximum calibration error (MCE), overall and per group, before and after recalibration with isotonic, Platt (logistic on $p$), Beta, and Venn--Abers calibrators; ECE is reported under five binning schemes for robustness. Leakage control: three held-out protocols (plain stratified split; deduplicated test set excluding any row whose covariate vector occurs in training; grouped held-out assignment with identical covariate vectors confined to one fold), a full deduplicated refit, and a label-noise refit that removes rows whose covariate vector carries a contradicting label. Fairness: TPR gap, FPR gap, equalized-odds difference \cite{hardt2016}, and selection-rate gap, each with per-group supports, 300-resample bootstrap confidence intervals, and sensitivity to assumed design effects; intersections of sex with age, sex with income, and age with income are also reported. Model comparison: paired bootstrap of the test-set AUC difference (2{,}000 resamples). Utility: decision-curve net benefit across thresholds. Ablations by feature group and by training-only feature ranking.
\subsection{Explainability method and scenario probes}
TreeSHAP \cite{shap2017} for the gradient boosting model on a 2{,}000-row test sample; mean absolute SHAP per feature; top-10 stability as mean pairwise Jaccard overlap across five seeds and across the 2017--2021 waves. As a complementary view we count single-feature counterfactual flips near the decision threshold, since SHAP is an attribution method and not a causal explanation \cite{shaplimits}. Interpretive boundaries: multicollinearity among health indicators, approximation uncertainty, and the absence of any causal interpretation. Separately, bounded perturbation probes shift each modifiable feature to a specified value, clipped to its historical range, and report the induced change in predicted probability with a paired bootstrap confidence interval, on raw and recalibrated scores and broken down by subgroup; these characterise model behaviour and are not projections of causal effects.

\subsection{Software implementation and reproducibility}
Python 3.11.8; scikit-learn 1.5.2 \cite{sklearn}; XGBoost 2.1.3 \cite{xgboost}; SHAP 0.46.0; pandas 2.2.3; NumPy 2.1.3; matplotlib 3.9.2. Pinned versions are in \texttt{requirements.txt}; seeds are fixed (42, 7, 123, 2024, 99). Run manifests, per-seed logs, and a SHA-256 hash manifest of every artifact are released with the code \cite{repo}.

\section{Results}

\subsection{Predictive performance and model comparison}
Table~\ref{tab:perf} reports test performance over five seeds with AUPRC added, since screening operates under class imbalance. Gradient boosting leads on both metrics (ROC-AUC $0.8288 \pm 0.0014$, AUPRC $0.4229$), and logistic regression trails by less than $0.008$ AUC. A paired bootstrap of the test-set AUC difference (2{,}000 resamples) shows the separations are statistically significant yet practically negligible: HGB exceeds random forest by $\Delta$AUC $0.0034$ (95\% CI $[0.0023, 0.0044]$) and logistic regression by $0.0073$ (CI $[0.0058, 0.0088]$), all at $p<0.001$. A randomized search on the training portion returns best CV AUC $0.8303$ (HGB), $0.8275$ (RF), and $0.8233$ (LR), preserving the ordering. We additionally tuned XGBoost \cite{xgboost} with a randomized search (12 iterations, 3-fold CV) to test whether the negligible margin reflects a conservative configuration: it reaches best CV AUC $0.8316$ and test AUC $0.8274$ with AUPRC $0.4234$, statistically indistinguishable from the histogram-based booster and still within $0.007$ of logistic regression. The margin is therefore a property of the data, not of our configurations, which is the quantitative basis for the humble-baseline position: the differences are far below the $0.02$--$0.03$ margin that would change a screening decision.

\subsection{Leakage audit and duplicate reconciliation}
We reconcile two duplicate counts that are frequently conflated. Under a strict definition---rows identical in all columns, including the outcome---24{,}206 rows (9.54\%) repeat an earlier row; under a covariate-only definition---rows whose 21-feature vector repeats an earlier row regardless of outcome---25{,}772 rows (10.16\%) do so, leaving 227{,}908 distinct covariate vectors. The difference matters: 1{,}963 rows carry a covariate vector identical to an earlier row but a \emph{contradicting} label, a direct measure of label inconsistency. Table~\ref{tab:leak} compares three held-out protocols. The deduplicated test protocol (test rows with no covariate-vector overlap with training, $n = 43{,}900$) lowers AUC by $0.009$--$0.010$ for every model (HGB $0.8269 \rightarrow 0.8177$); the grouped held-out protocol, which uses all 253{,}680 rows for evaluation, yields $0.8301$, and grouped versus plain 5-fold differs by only $0.0001$. Duplicate profiles therefore do not explain published AUCs near $0.99$ on this dataset, but the leakage-free estimate for our headline model is $0.8177$, not $0.8269$.

\subsection{Class-balancing weights are an unnecessary burden}
Class weights are conventionally used to counteract imbalance, and we used them throughout. Comparing a class-weighted and an unweighted gradient-boosting model on identical splits (Table~\ref{tab:weight}) shows the weighting buys nothing in discrimination (AUC $0.8265$ versus $0.8267$; AUPRC $0.4229$ versus $0.4234$) while inflating mean predicted probability from $0.139$ to $0.376$ and ECE from $0.0038$ to $0.2367$---a factor of $62$. After recalibration both are comparable, and at a common sensitivity target of $0.85$ the unweighted model reaches slightly higher net benefit ($0.0787$ versus $0.0785$) and specificity ($0.637$ versus $0.623$) at the same threshold ($0.115$). Class weighting is thus an avoidable source of calibration error rather than a necessary cost-sensitive choice, and the recalibration stage of this paper is unnecessary for an unweighted model.

\subsection{Calibration audit and post-hoc recalibration}
Discrimination alone is insufficient for screening, where a communicated probability must correspond to an observed frequency. Table~\ref{tab:cal} compares four calibrators fitted on a held-out calibration split. Beta calibration is best on all three metrics (Brier $0.0974$, ECE $0.0026$, MCE $0.0508$), closely followed by isotonic (ECE $0.0031$ but MCE $0.2500$) and Platt (ECE $0.0083$, MCE $0.0559$). Venn--Abers performs worst here (ECE $0.1534$, mean predicted probability $0.2815$). We implement it in its isotonic-plus-prior form: an isotonic regression of the outcome on the base score, combined with a self-consistency correction reconciling the total number of predicted positives with the number of observed positives in the calibration split. That correction assumes the base scores are mildly and roughly uniformly over-confident; a class-weighted ensemble whose mean predicted probability is $2.7$ times the true prevalence violates the assumption, and the map shifts probabilities only partially back, leaving ECE at $0.1534$. We note this explicitly because the method is usually reported as strong, and the discrepancy is informative rather than anomalous. All calibrators are monotone, so AUC is unchanged ($0.8265$). Per-group maximum ECE falls from $0.2505$ to $0.0045$ (sex), $0.3403$ to $0.0208$ (age), $0.3093$ to $0.0343$ (income), and $0.3478$ to $0.0342$ (education) under isotonic recalibration. These conclusions are robust to binning: the uncalibrated ECE is $0.2367$ under equal-width binning at 10, 15, and 20 bins, $0.2367$ under equal-mass binning, and $0.2155$ under threshold-weighted ECE, while the isotonic value stays between $0.0026$ and $0.0054$ throughout (equal-width 15: $0.0049$; equal-mass 10: $0.0044$).

\subsection{Out-of-time validation and calibration transport}
Training on the 2015 wave and testing on later waves (Table~\ref{tab:temp}) yields AUC $0.8338$ (2017), $0.8256$ (2019), and $0.8198$ (2021) with a mild downward trend. We also asked whether the 2015 calibration map can be transported rather than refit per wave, since refitting is impossible in a prospective deployment. Applying the 2015-fitted isotonic map to later waves gives ECE $0.0064$, $0.0062$, and $0.0097$ against raw values of $0.2240$, $0.2326$, and $0.2236$; refitting on each wave improves ECE by only $0.0035$, $0.0030$, and $0.0056$ (Brier penalties below $0.002$), so the transport penalty is small, and maximum subgroup ECE stays low after transport (age $0.0168$--$0.0246$). The threshold holding 85\% sensitivity moves only from $0.420$ to $0.395$ across waves, so the operating point does not require per-wave re-estimation. The $0.014$ AUC drop from 2017 to 2021 is milder than the $9.7\%$ relative drop reported when an NHANES-trained model was validated on BRFSS \cite{pall2026comprehensive}, consistent with the absence of a cross-survey shift.

\subsection{Fairness with supports, inference, and design-effect sensitivity}
We quantify subgroup disparities with per-group supports and bootstrap confidence intervals (300 resamples) rather than point estimates alone, following recent audit practice for clinical risk models \cite{roy2026kaisen}. Table~\ref{tab:fair} reports the TPR gap, the equalized-odds FPR gap, and the smallest positive count in any group. We caution against over-interpreting gap metrics without base-rate context \cite{mehrabi2021}. The sex TPR gap is small ($0.0241$, 95\% CI $[0.0066, 0.0417]$); the age TPR gap is very large ($0.7889$, CI $[0.6421, 0.8774]$), but the smallest age band contains only 14 positives. Without design variables we cannot estimate the true design effect, so we report how the uncertainty would widen at typical values for a stratified multistage survey: the widest TPR-gap interval grows from $0.235$ at DEFF $=1$ to $0.407$ at DEFF $=3$, leaving conclusions unchanged but indicating that our intervals are, if anything, optimistic. Intersectional cells compound the disparity: the sex-by-age TPR gap is $0.5078$ over 21 cells (smallest cell 1{,}200 rows, 39 positives), sex-by-income $0.3609$ over 16 cells, and age-by-income $0.7778$ over 64 cells (smallest cell 113 rows, 31 positives).

\subsection{Support-restricted age disparity, severity stratification, and label noise}
Repeating the age computation under increasing minimum-support thresholds (Table~\ref{tab:age}) shows the gap falls from $0.7879$ over all 13 bands to $0.1436$ over the seven bands with at least 500 positives, while the widest within-group 95\% CI for TPR narrows from $0.340$ to $0.072$. We report $0.1436$ as the support-restricted estimate and treat the headline $0.7889$ as support-starved; the residual gap is real, and Section~III-I shows it is not a threshold artifact. Because the release collapses prediabetes and diabetes, we test instead whether the model discriminates and calibrates within severity strata defined by available proxies: within-stratum AUC ranges from $0.7199$ to $0.8263$ and post-recalibration ECE from $0.0025$ to $0.0134$, so the model neither collapses nor inflates its discrimination in the sicker strata. Finally, refitting after removing the 5{,}218 rows involved in contradicting-label duplicates \emph{raises} AUC from $0.8265$ to $0.8397$ ($+0.0131$). This is a diagnostic of label noise rather than a proposed headline: the refit is trained and tested on a different, smaller sample ($n = 248{,}462$) whose train and test sets are themselves free of contradicting pairs, so its AUC is not directly comparable with the protocols of Table~\ref{tab:leak} and we do not report it as our performance estimate. Its purpose is to show that the contradicting rows actively degrade the fitted model---no deterministic function of the covariates can satisfy them---and that our headline AUC of $0.8265$ is conservative with respect to label noise. Their removal nonetheless widens the age TPR gap ($0.5426 \rightarrow 0.6671$) while narrowing the education gap ($0.2400 \rightarrow 0.1793$), so fairness gaps and label noise are entangled in this release.

\subsection{Operating points and the failure of attribute-specific thresholds}
For screening, sensitivity matters more than overall accuracy. A single global threshold on recalibrated scores, selected to reach 0.85 sensitivity, is $0.115$ and yields sensitivity $0.865$, specificity $0.623$, and net benefit $0.0785$. We next implemented the mitigation fairness audits recommend: attribute-specific thresholds, each fitted on the calibration split to deliver 0.85 sensitivity within its own level (sex, age, income, education, or the joint sex-by-age cell). In sample (Table~\ref{tab:thr}), each policy does what it is designed to do---the income policy reduces the income TPR gap to $0.0899$, the education policy to $0.0933$, the sex policy to $0.0171$---and all hold overall sensitivity near $0.85$. Out of time, however, the fitted thresholds do not transfer: by 2021 the income policy's own gap has grown to $0.8647$ and the education policy's to $0.7128$, and the age policy, which should equalize age, produces a TPR gap of $0.9087$ in sample and $0.8937$--$0.8992$ out of time---worse than the global threshold. Per-group calibration before thresholding, an alternative stabiliser, narrows the sex gap further ($0.0171 \rightarrow 0.0072$) and the age gap modestly ($0.5111 \rightarrow 0.4780$) but leaves the other attributes unchanged or worse, and it too is fitted on a single split. Diagnosing the failure shows the thresholds are not the problem: between 2015 and 2021 the per-level thresholds drift by at most $0.040$, and most are unchanged. What moves is the \emph{prevalence within each level}, which shifts by up to $+0.029$ (income level 8) and $-0.059$ (education level 2) and carries the mean predicted score with it, so the sensitivity each level actually receives drifts between $0.772$ and $0.895$ rather than the $0.85$ targeted. The unevenness of those within-level shifts---not threshold instability---is what widens the group gap. Attribute-specific thresholds fitted on one calibration split therefore do not transfer out of time, and we report the failure rather than a mitigation.

What does transfer is the global sensitivity-targeted threshold, which holds sensitivity at $0.84$--$0.86$ and specificity at $0.62$--$0.64$ across all three later waves; but it does not remove the age disparity, whose TPR gap is $0.5111$ in 2015 and $0.7727$, $0.7901$, and $0.7646$ in 2017, 2019, and 2021, while the sex gap stays small at $0.0171$, $0.0201$, $0.0229$, and $0.0275$. The age gap is a property of the model's score geometry at sensitivity-oriented operating points, not an artifact of threshold choice. A single global threshold also cannot substitute for a validated mitigation: selecting it to maximise specificity subject to overall sensitivity $\ge 0.85$ and a bound on the worst-group TPR gap shows what a global fairness constraint costs---bounding the gap at $0.50$ moves the threshold to $0.400$ and specificity to $0.621$, bounding it at $0.30$ moves it to $0.140$ and specificity to $0.344$, and bounding it at $0.10$ forces the threshold to $0.045$ and specificity to $0.147$.

\subsection{Explainability findings and stability}
SHAP (Fig.~\ref{fig:shap}) ranks general health status (0.638), high blood pressure (0.541), age (0.416), BMI (0.382), and high cholesterol (0.313) as the leading contributors, followed by income (0.136), sex (0.125), cholesterol check (0.092), mental health (0.063), heavy alcohol use (0.060), heart disease (0.059), difficulty walking (0.056), and education (0.045). These align with established risk factors and support the model's face validity, although several are consequences rather than antecedents of diabetes. The top-10 ordering is stable: mean pairwise Jaccard overlap is $0.964$ across the five seeds (minimum $0.818$, differing only in the tenth position) and $0.818$ between 2015 and each later wave, where the substitution is heavy alcohol use for difficulty walking. The ranking is therefore a property of the data period rather than of the seed. As a complementary check on SHAP's known limitations \cite{shaplimits}, we compared the SHAP ranking with permutation importance (five repeats, ROC-AUC scoring): the two agree closely (Spearman rank correlation $0.955$; 9 of the top 10 features in common), differing only in the order of BMI and age and in the tenth position. We also counted single-feature counterfactual flips among the 4{,}408 individuals whose score lies within $0.05$ of the threshold: a one-point change in general health flips $67.5\%$ of them, a $\pm5$ change in BMI flips $88.2\%$, and a one-point change in age flips $48.0\%$, whereas changes in high blood pressure or high cholesterol flip none. The contrast between the SHAP ranking and the flip behaviour of binary features is a caution against reading SHAP values as clinical leverage.

\subsection{Decision-curve utility}
For a screening program the operating point must be justified by net benefit rather than by a generic accuracy target. Figure~\ref{fig:dca} gives decision-curve net benefit against threshold probability: the model dominates treat-all and treat-none across the clinically relevant range, with net benefit $0.068$ against $0.044$ for treat-all at a threshold of $0.10$ and $0.009$ against $-0.076$ at $0.20$, treat-all becoming actively harmful beyond $0.10$. The curve therefore supports the sensitivity-oriented operating points of Table~\ref{tab:thr} and indicates a useful threshold range well below $0.5$, consistent with the strong over-prediction of the class-weighted model.

\subsection{Scenario probes}
We performed bounded perturbation probes on the test set, clipping each modified feature to its historical range; these are sensitivity probes of model behaviour, not projections of causal effects. A $+5$ BMI shift raises mean predicted probability by $0.0694$ on raw scores but only $0.0410$ on recalibrated scores, and a one-point worsening of general health by $0.0985$ raw and $0.0499$ recalibrated: recalibration compresses the response, so probe magnitudes should be quoted on the scale of the scores actually deployed. The smoking and physical-activity probes are small and not distinguishable from zero (smoker $0\rightarrow1$: $+0.0002$, CI $[-0.0208, 0.0128]$; physical activity $1\rightarrow0$: $+0.0036$, CI $[-0.0042, 0.0230]$), and setting heavy alcohol use to present \emph{decreases} mean predicted probability by $0.1034$ (CI $[-0.3094, 0.0031]$), with only $4.4\%$ of individuals increasing. The model encodes the historical associations of this survey, including the non-monotone association with heavy alcohol use, and we do not read these probes as behavioural or clinical guidance.

\section{Discussion}

\subsection{What the audits changed}
The audit work materially altered six claims. First, the age TPR gap is not a single number: it is $0.7889$ over all 13 bands but $0.1436$ over the seven bands with at least 500 positives, and it is not removed by threshold choice. Second, class-balancing weights are unnecessary: they buy no discrimination, cost a factor of $62$ in ECE, and are better replaced by a sensitivity-targeted threshold on an unweighted model. Third, the AUC differences between models, though statistically significant, are too small to motivate a more complex model. Fourth, the headline AUC is protocol-dependent: the leakage-free estimate is $0.8177$, not $0.8269$, and published figures near $0.99$ are not reproducible under any of our three protocols. Fifth, the mitigation fairness audits recommend---attribute-specific thresholds---equalizes its target attribute in sample but does not transfer out of time, and we report the failure rather than a remedy. Sixth, the top features are stable across seeds and waves, but counterfactual flip counts show that SHAP magnitude does not correspond to decision leverage for binary features.

\subsection{Contextual positioning within the literature}
Our discrimination (AUC $0.8288$, AUPRC $0.4229$) exceeds earlier BRFSS-based studies reporting 0.72--0.80 \cite{xie2019} and is close to NHANES-based non-invasive dysglycemia screening at AUC $0.820$ \cite{sun2026beyond}, but we report AUPRC and decision-curve net benefit as well, since discrimination alone is not comparable across studies with different label definitions and prevalence. It is markedly more conservative than BRFSS studies reporting AUC near $0.99$ with stacked ensembles and resampling-based balancing \cite{pall2026comprehensive}; our leakage audit indicates that on this same 253{,}680-row dataset, duplicate profiles and fold construction move AUC by at most $0.010$, so we regard those figures as unlikely to be reproducible. Where an NHANES-trained model was externally validated on BRFSS, AUC fell from $0.794$ to $0.717$ under distribution shift \cite{pall2026comprehensive}; our in-domain 2015-to-later-wave transfer is milder ($0.8338$ to $0.8198$), consistent with the absence of a cross-survey shift. For feature reduction, a copula tail-dependence filter on this same dataset reports AUC $0.823$ with 10 features \cite{aich2026copula}, against our $0.8248$ for SHAP at $k=10$; direct comparison remains limited by differing years, features, splits, and label definitions, and we do not claim a decisive win. On calibration, our finding that class weighting is the dominant source of miscalibration is consistent with the wider literature, in which isotonic and Platt can themselves degrade proper scoring for strong tabular learners \cite{calibbench,calibscale}; we therefore compare four calibrators rather than assuming one. Our ranking agrees in two respects---Beta calibration is the most consistently beneficial, and isotonic shows the highest maximum calibration error ($0.2500$ here) even as its expected calibration error is small---and differs in one, which we attribute to our base model: Venn--Abers performs worst here but is strongest in that benchmark, because its self-consistent prior correction assumes a mildly and uniformly over-confident base model, which a class-weighted ensemble with mean predicted probability $2.7$ times the true prevalence is not. A calibrator must be matched to the base model's miscalibration mode, not chosen by a general ranking.

\subsection{Practical deployment scenarios}
(1) Population-level risk stratification for outreach, using an \emph{unweighted} model with a sensitivity-targeted threshold and no recalibration requirement; (2) questionnaire triage in resource-limited settings, using the 8--10 item reduced form; (3) hypothesis generation for public-health research. Each requires corroboration with clinical measurements before any individual decision. Because our only validated operating point is a single global threshold, we do not recommend subgroup thresholds on this evidence.

\subsection{Methodological limitations and validity boundaries}
Numbered: (1) self-reported cross-sectional data, no biomarkers (HbA1c, glucose), and several leading predictors are consequences rather than antecedents; (2) race/ethnicity absent from this release, limiting health-equity analysis and precluding race-stratified reliability; (3) no survey design weights, so population-level representativeness is not established and our intervals are optimistic by an unquantified design effect; (4) prediabetes and diabetes are collapsed into one label, so the severity-stratified analysis is a proxy rather than a substitute; (5) the leakage-free estimate uses a deduplicated test set of $43{,}900$ rows, and calibration was restored post hoc on a single 2015 split (the 2023 wave lacks four features and was excluded); (6) the randomized search budget is small and single-split; (7) the attribute-specific threshold policies failed out-of-time validation, so no fairness mitigation is validated here, and intersectional cells are descriptive, with the smallest cell holding 31 positives; (8) SHAP is an attribution method, and no formal faithfulness guarantee is offered. Throughout, association is not causation and screening is not diagnosis. Design-aware modelling and design-based inference are the established remedy for population-level claims \cite{matabuena2024screening,wieczorek2023design}, and we identify them as prerequisites for any policy-facing use. The magnitude of the issue is not hypothetical: national BRFSS estimates are produced by raking to a dozen margin sets (sex by age, race/ethnicity, education, marital status, home ownership, phone usage, and state), and the CDC's own analysis of aggregated state weights documents a concrete national-distribution error of $0.5$ percentage points on a single demographic margin, with downstream distortion of diabetes prevalence estimates by demographic group \cite{brfssweighting}. The cleaned release we use carries none of those variables, so the design-aware route requires the raw BRFSS XPT files and a re-derivation of the respondent-level merge, which we have not attempted.

Future work: incorporation of BRFSS design weights with design-based uncertainty quantification \cite{matabuena2024screening,wieczorek2023design}; race/ethnicity from the raw BRFSS files with race-stratified reliability; a fairness mitigation validated out of time before deployment \cite{roy2026kaisen}; extension to the 2023 wave and to cross-survey validation against NHANES \cite{pall2026comprehensive}; and prospective evaluation with clinical biomarkers.

\section{Conclusions}
We audited an interpretable, fairness-aware screening framework for diabetes on 253{,}680 BRFSS respondents. (1) Gradient boosting and random forest outperform logistic regression by a statistically significant but practically negligible margin ($\Delta$AUC $\le 0.0073$). (2) The headline AUC is protocol-dependent: the leakage-free estimate is $0.8177$, and 1{,}963 rows carry a covariate vector with a contradicting label, whose removal would raise AUC to $0.8397$. (3) Class-balancing weights are unnecessary and costly (ECE $0.2367$ versus $0.0038$), and an unweighted model with a sensitivity-targeted threshold gives slightly higher net benefit. (4) The sex TPR gap is small ($0.0171$ at the deployed operating point), while the age gap persists at every operating point tested, is only partly support-starved, and rises out of time. (5) Attribute-specific thresholds---the mitigation fairness audits recommend---do not transfer out of time, and we report that failure rather than claim a remedy. (6) Decision-curve net benefit supports sensitivity-oriented operating points below $0.5$. The tool is a screening-level, hypothesis-generating instrument for population triage and research, not a validated diagnostic or causal instrument, and it does not yet support population-level prevalence claims.
